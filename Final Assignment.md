### The Game Trailer and Technology

OBSCURITY combines game and education, focusing on an abstract portrayal of cookies, algorithms and other kinds of ‘digital surveillance’.
In OBSCURITY the player needs to click (randomly spawning) camera icons across their screen, before the cameras despawn naturally, to prevent their Obscurity rating from falling. If the rating drops too low, the player is prevented from closing pop-up windows, which appear in the style of advertisements, cookies or (mock) login pages, which in turn further decrease the rating, if not closed on time by the player.
The main gameplay loop is intended to invoke a feeling of stress and paranoia, with the spawnrate of cameras increasing not only over time, but also through increased player-screen interaction. The more the player does on their screen, whether it is searching in the search-bar, accessing a website or simply interacting with the homescreen, cameras appear on screen more frequently. Rather than offering a proper ending to the game, OBSCURITY provides replayability by providing an individual leaderboard, challenging the player to survive longer than in the previous run. This mirrors reality, in that protecting your data remains a constant fight, rather than something you can permanently achieve.
An interesting thought might be to have the cameras spawn intentionally, for example when detecting advertisement and especially tracking, maybe tied to an adblocker. This however, goes far beyond what I am currently able to produce and work out.


Not only is OBSCURITY dependent on and connected with technology by virtue of being a game (concept) and living off of user interaction, but the process of making the project, the game trailer, involved a lot of technology as well.
In combining traditional ink and digital art, screen recording and editing, the trailer has a unique style. It forced me to go out of my comfort zone, usually animating frame-by-frame and including a handful of keyframe animations.
The designs were originally conceptualised with ink on paper, before being digitalised. During this process, some designs were tweaked for better recognisability. The assets remain hand-drawn on tablet, using ToonSquid for iPad.
The trailer includes audio-edited material, a small part of which is entirely self-recorded, namely the Undertale-inspired talking sound effect. Audio editing was done via Audacity and LMMS.
While not coding the entire game, mainly due to time restraints, I took some notes and conceptualised potential functions in hopes of revisiting the project in the future and maybe producing an extended demo and later a full game experience.
Some rough code-scaffolding, for the transparent, non-interactable overlay was coded in Python via PyCharm.


### Connection To In-Class Material

Most people have encountered it before: Five minutes ago you mentioned needing to buy a new dog-toy and all of a sudden your phone offers a variety of high-end dog-toys, most likely fitting your dog’s breed and in your favourite colours.
Most people, however are not aware of the reason for this. Say you save an image on Pinterest to your device, rather than pin it inside the app, this tells the Pinterest algorithm “I am especially interested in images like this” and therefore, your next recommendations may very likely resemble that image in particular. Cookies to gather data for better advertisement, IP-addresses to track, where an order or search was made and by which device, browsing-histories to make better suggestions to what you might look for next.


If you visit the website of a hotel in Amsterdam repeatedly, let alone book a room, rest assured, your next Google Search will advertise an array of events or places to visit for your vacation. If you follow a demonstration on any social media, or visit their webpage, authorities might keep an eye on you, or worse, if said demonstration might ever be illegalised by a change in government, for example. Equally, leaving your phone at home when going out, while usually taking it with you everywhere, creates a gap in your location, that might even incriminate you, if a crime happens to take place during this time.
All this may be called “Big Data”, as described by Shoshana Zuboff in “Big other: surveillance capitalism and the prospects of an information civilization”, data, which might be used against you in a variety of ways and may violate your privacy (though, as mentioned by Zuboff, privacy is a hard to determine and harder to define concept).


OBSCURITY is supposed to present some of these going-ons in a gamified way. Especially important for this show-and-tell system is the game’s mascot Anon, a shorthand for the word anonymous, often used in online spaces, who comments on the player’s actions and potential carelessness with their data.
To implement this, it would be most effective, if Anon were to “react” to actual user action, outside the direct game, such as accepting cookies on a website or creating a new account for a website, though this of course comes with its own data and privacy problems and I personally would be unsure on how something like this might be possible. Maybe, if the game were able to “read” the screen, without saving any data, merely scanning for strings, such as “accept cookies”, “accept all”, “register email”, or similar, though of course this system would not be entirely reliable.
Similarly, if the game were to recognise actions taken in order to protect data – eg. activating a VPN, enabling an ad- or popup-blocker, etc – a reflection and comments by Anon, as well as a reflection of positive, or negative action on the Obscurity rating, would enhance the gameplay and educational value.
Alternatively, a similar feel might be achieved by having the previously mentioned level-up menu, as well as more specialised mock pop-ups.


In “Obfuscation: A User’s Guide For Privacy And Protest”, Finn Brunton and Helen Nissenbaum describe actions that may be taken by a user to protect their data. The plans brought forward might act as qualitative inspiration for the game’s skills and level-up features. One way to implement obfuscation, as described by the authors could be through minigames. Small challenges, unlocking obfuscation methods. They would focus on obscuring, veiling, and replacing data, rather than deleting and fully hiding it and include the purposes presented in chapter 5.2. Buying time, providing cover, granting deniability, denying exposure, interfering with profiling and expressing protest are the reasons, or goals, presented in the text. Especially interesting to portray in a game like OBSCURITY would be the aspects of time, cover and exposure, all of which capable of impacting gameplay.

OBSCURITY is my (small) attempt at combining information on digital surveillance, a topic not too often discussed, at least outside my immediate friend circles – as I noticed, when talking with some family-friends, who seemed to have barely any grasp on what their digital footprint might be – and a tiny touch of hacktivism, a topic I find highly fascinating.
